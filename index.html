Life in the UK — Study Notes

Lesson 1 — Values and Principles of the UK

Fundamental Principles of British Life

  • Individual liberty

Responsibilities as a British Citizen

  • Respect and obey the law
  • Look after yourself and your family

Freedoms the UK Offers

  • Freedom of speech
  • Freedom of belief in religion
Questions on this section are straightforward — the correct answer is usually the most obvious / common-sense option among the multiple choices.

Lesson 2 — What Is the UK

The United Kingdom

  • Made up of 4 countries: England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland
  • Full name: The United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland
  • Great Britain = England + Scotland + Wales (NOT Northern Ireland)
  • Governed by Parliament sitting in Westminster (central London)

Crown Dependencies (linked to UK but NOT part of the UK)

  • Channel Islands (in the English Channel between England and France)
  • Isle of Man (island off the coast of England)

British Overseas Territories (linked to UK but NOT part of the UK)

  • Saint Helena (small island off the coast of Africa)
  • Falkland Islands (off the coast of Argentina)
Crown dependencies and British overseas territories are NOT part of the UK.

Lesson 3 — Early Britain

Stone Age

  • First people in Britain were hunter-gatherers
  • Came via a land bridge connecting mainland Europe to Britain
  • ~10,000 years ago: Britain permanently separated by the Channel
  • First farmers arrived ~6,000 years ago, most likely from southeast Europe
  • Famous Stone Age sites:
    • Stonehenge — in Wiltshire, England (arrangement of giant rocks, used for seasonal ceremonies)
    • Skara Brae — on Orkney, off coast of mainland Scotland (well-preserved prehistoric village)

Bronze Age (~4,000 years ago)

  • People started using bronze
  • Lived in round houses
  • Buried their dead in round barrows

Iron Age

  • People made tools and weapons from iron
  • First coins minted in Britain — inscribed with names of Iron Age kings
  • Lived in round houses grouped into hill forts
  • Maiden Castle = impressive hill fort still visible today
  • Language spoken: Celtic

Romans

  • 55 BC: Julius Caesar leads unsuccessful invasion
  • AD 43: Emperor Claudius leads successful invasion
  • Boudicca (Boadicea): famous tribal leader who rebelled against the Romans
    • Statue of her on Westminster Bridge by Houses of Parliament
  • Hadrian's Wall: built by Emperor Hadrian to keep out the Picts (ancestors of Scottish people)
    • Forts of Housesteads and Vindolanda can still be seen today
  • Romans built roads, public buildings, created structure of law, introduced new plants and animals
  • AD 410: Romans leave Britain (after ~400 years)

Anglo-Saxons

  • After Romans left, Britain invaded by Jutes, Angles, and Saxons (from northern Europe)
  • By AD 600: Anglo-Saxon kingdoms established
  • Sutton Hoo: famous burial place of an Anglo-Saxon king (buried with treasure and armor in a ship)

Early Christian Missionaries

  • Arrived in 3rd and 4th centuries to preach Christianity
  • Three famous missionaries:
    • Saint Patrick (from Ireland)
    • Saint Columba (from Ireland)
    • Saint Augustine (from Rome) — became the FIRST Archbishop of Canterbury

Vikings (from AD 789)

  • Came from Denmark, Norway, and Sweden
  • Anglo-Saxons united under King Alfred the Great, who defeated the Vikings
  • In the north, people united under Kenneth MacAlpin — term "Scotland" used for that country
  • Danish kings briefly ruled England; first Danish king was Knut (Canute)

Norman Conquest (1066)

  • William, Duke of Normandy (from northern France) invades Britain
  • Defeats King Harold at the Battle of Hastings in 1066
  • Earns title "William the Conqueror"
  • This was the LAST successful foreign invasion of Britain
  • Bayeux Tapestry: embroidery (~70 meters long) commemorating the battle, still in France today
  • Domesday Book: lists of people, land, and animals they owned — still exists today

Lesson 4 — The Middle Ages

Wars and Conflicts

  • Wales: 1284 — English annexed Wales under the Statute of Rhuddlan
  • Scotland: 1314 — Robert the Bruce defeated the English at the Battle of Bannockburn
  • Ireland: By 1200 — English established rule in the Pale (area just outside Dublin)
  • Abroad:
    • The Crusades — war for control of the Holy Land
    • The Hundred Years War (actually 116 years) with France
      • Battle of Agincourt: Henry V defeats a much larger French army

Feudalism

  • System established by the Normans
  • King → Lords (given land in return for help in war) → Serfs (peasants who worked the land)
  • Serfs = regular peasants who worked for their lord and fought when the king requested

The Black Death (1348)

  • A plague that killed around one third of the population
  • Led to labour shortages → peasants demanded higher wages
  • Resulted in emergence of a middle class and large landowners called "gentry"

Legal and Political Changes

  • Magna Carta (1215) = "Great Charter"
    • Charter of rights that noblemen pressured king to accept
    • Established that even the king was subject to the law (limited king's power)
  • Parliament developed during this period with two houses:
    • House of Lords: lords, bishops, great landowners
    • House of Commons: knights, small landowners, wealthy townspeople
  • Scotland had its own parliament with three houses: Lords, Commons, and Clergy
  • English law: Common law — based on precedent (following previous decisions)
  • Scottish law: Codified (written down)

Language and Culture

  • Anglo-Saxon (peasants) + Norman French (king and nobles) = Modern English
  • By 1400: English used in official documents, royal court, and parliament
  • In Scotland: people continued to speak Gaelic
  • Geoffrey Chaucer wrote The Canterbury Tales (poems about pilgrims traveling to Canterbury)
    • One of the first books printed by William Caxton using the printing press
  • John Barbour wrote The Bruce (poems about Battle of Bannockburn, written in Gaelic)

Lesson 5 — The Tudors and Stuarts

Wars of the Roses (1455–1485)

  • Civil war over who should be king of England
  • House of Lancaster (red rose) led by Henry Tudor vs House of York (white rose) led by Richard III
  • Henry Tudor wins, kills Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth (1485)
  • Henry Tudor marries Richard III's niece to unite families
  • Becomes Henry VII, first king of the House of Tudor
  • Tudor Rose = red (Lancaster) + white (York)

Henry VIII

  • Son of Henry VII
  • Broke away from the Church of Rome (Pope refused divorce from first wife)
  • Established the Church of England (king holds power, not the Pope)
  • Married 6 times:
    1. Catherine of Aragon — Spanish princess, gave him daughter Mary, divorced
    2. Anne Boleyn — gave him daughter Elizabeth, executed
    3. Jane Seymour — gave him son Edward (the heir he wanted), died after birth
    4. Anne of Cleves — German princess, political marriage, divorced quickly
    5. Catherine Howard — cousin of Anne Boleyn, executed
    6. Catherine Parr — widow, survived Henry, remarried after he died
  • During his reign: Wales formally united with England (Act of Government of Wales)

The Reformation

  • Movement across Europe against the authority of the Pope and Roman Catholic Church
  • Protestants: formed own churches, read Bible in own language, believed personal relationship with God more important than church authority
  • English tried to impose Protestant ideas in Ireland → led to rebellion

Edward VI

  • Son of Henry VIII, died age 15, short reign (6 years)
  • Introduced the Book of Common Prayer (Church of England, still used today)

Bloody Mary (Mary I)

  • Half-sister of Edward VI, devout Catholic
  • Persecuted Protestants (hence nickname "Bloody Mary")
  • Short reign

Elizabeth I

  • Protestant, very successful monarch
  • Balanced Catholic and extreme Protestant views (avoided serious religious conflict)
  • Skilled at managing Parliament
  • Defeated the Spanish Armada (1588) — large fleet sent by Spain to conquer England and restore Catholicism
  • Elizabethan period = growing patriotism, new trade routes, colonisation of east coast of America began
  • Sir Francis Drake: led defeat of Spanish Armada, circumnavigated the world on the Golden Hind
  • William Shakespeare: plays, poems, and sonnets
    • Famous plays: A Midsummer Night's Dream, Hamlet, Macbeth, Romeo and Juliet
Tudor Succession: Henry VII → Henry VIII → Edward VI → Bloody Mary → Elizabeth I

Mary Queen of Scots (Mary Stuart)

  • Catholic, crowned queen at one week old
  • Spent most of her childhood in France
  • Accused of killing her husband → fled to England, gave throne to Protestant son James VI
  • Cousin of Elizabeth I
  • Imprisoned for 20 years by Elizabeth I, then executed (accused of plotting for English throne)

James I of England (James VI of Scotland)

  • Son of Mary Queen of Scots, cousin of Elizabeth I
  • Elizabeth I had no children → James became king of both England and Scotland
  • Scotland still remained a separate country
  • Developed the King James Bible (also known as the Authorized Version)
  • Believed in the Divine Right of Kings (king appointed by God, no need for Parliament approval)

Ireland during Tudor/Stuart Period

  • Henry VIII took title "King of Ireland", extended control beyond the Pale
  • Rebellions against Protestant/English rule under Elizabeth I and James I
  • Plantations: English and Scottish Protestants settled in Ulster (northern Ireland), took land from Catholic landowners

Charles I

  • Son of James I, also believed in Divine Right of Kings
  • Ruled without Parliament for 11 years
  • Puritans: strict Protestants who disagreed with Charles's religious views

English Civil War

  • Cavaliers (supporters of the King) vs Roundheads (supporters of Parliament)
  • Charles I defeated at Battle of Marston Moor and Battle of Naseby
  • Charles I executed in 1649

The Commonwealth (Republic)

  • England declared a republic after Charles I's execution
  • Scotland declared Charles II (son of Charles I) as their king
  • Charles II leads army into England, defeated at Battles of Dunbar and Worcester
  • Charles II escapes to Europe (famously hid in an oak tree)
  • Oliver Cromwell: general who defeated Charles II, given title Lord Protector

The Restoration (1660)

  • After 11 years as republic + death of Cromwell, people wanted a king again
  • Parliament invites Charles II back from exile to be king
Restoration (re-establishing monarchy) is NOT the same as Reformation (movement against the Pope).

During Charles II's Reign

  • 1665: Major plague in London
  • 1666: Great Fire of London
  • 1679: Habeas Corpus Act — illegal to hold prisoner without a court hearing
  • Royal Society established (oldest surviving science society)
    • Isaac Newton — discovered gravity
    • Sir Edmund Halley — predicted return of Halley's Comet

The Glorious Revolution

  • Charles II had no legitimate children → brother James II (Catholic) becomes king
  • James favoured Catholics, causing unrest
  • William of Orange (Protestant, from Netherlands, married to James's daughter Mary)
  • Protestants invited William to invade England
  • No fighting when William arrived → called the Glorious Revolution
  • James flees to France, returns with French army, defeated at Battle of the Boyne, flees again
  • Scottish clans required to accept William; McDonalds of Glencoe were late taking oath → massacred
  • Jacobites: Scots who still supported James

Lesson 6 — Arts and Culture

Music

  • The Proms: 8-week summer season of orchestral classical music, organized by the BBC
  • Major outdoor music festivals: Glastonbury, Isle of Wight, Creamfields
  • Mercury Prize: awarded each year for best album in UK and Ireland
  • Brit Awards: annual awards covering a range of music categories

Theatre

  • Theatreland: area in London's West End with many theatres
  • Gilbert and Sullivan: famous for writing comic operas (made fun of popular culture and politics)
  • Pantomime: British tradition, fairy stories / light-hearted plays staged at Christmas time
  • Edinburgh Fringe Festival: important festival showcasing theatre from across the country
  • Laurence Olivier Awards: annual award for variety of theatre categories

Art

  • Famous London galleries: National Gallery, Tate Britain, Tate Modern
  • Turner Prize: annual award for contemporary art at Tate Britain

Architecture

  • Gothic style: famous buildings include Houses of Parliament and St Pancras Station
  • Sir Edwin Lutyens: famous British architect
    • Designed New Delhi seat in India
    • Designed the Cenotaph in Whitehall, London (war memorial)
  • Chelsea Flower Show: showcases garden design from Britain and around the world

Literature

  • Several British writers have won the Nobel Prize in Literature
  • Man Booker Prize: awarded for best fiction novel by author from the Commonwealth, Ireland, or Zimbabwe
  • Key writers: Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, JK Rowling, JRR Tolkien, Sir Arthur Conan Doyle
  • Famous poets: William Blake, Lord Byron, Robert Browning
